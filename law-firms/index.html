What the week looks like

You already know where the hours go

None of this is on anyone's list of problems. When we ask people what the worst part of their week is, the word that comes back is tedious.

01

We are still scheduling mediations by email

Someone proposes dates. People reply slowly. The dates expire and you start again.

What changes

One link replaces the whole exchange. Everyone marks what works, you confirm, it locks. This one is free and you can have it today.

02

We have a forms folder, and nobody trusts it

So people go hunting. A recent case they know was done properly, the person at the next desk, or memory. Then they save a private copy, and now there are two. Do that for three years and nobody can say which one is current, or who owns it.

What changes

One hub carries the current version of each document, with who approved it and when, because that is what people check before they use one. New drafts get built from the case information, with AI doing the first pass. Your team reviews and sends.

03

We know we are leaving billing on the table

Six minutes a day, per timekeeper, is more than four thousand dollars a year at a $175 rate. It goes because time gets written on paper and retyped by somebody else, and because a hundred emails a day have to be read, saved and then billed. By Friday the week is being rebuilt from memory.

What changes

We redesign the flow so time gets captured where the work happens and the double handling goes. The person who writes on paper keeps writing on paper, and their hours still reach the system.

04

One person knows where everything stands

Ask her about a records request and she knows, because she is carrying forty matters in her head.

What changes

That moves into something the whole team reads and updates. Nobody emails for status, and nothing walks out of the door with the person.

05

The trial calendar is a Word document

Someone maintains it by hand, it is out of date the day it is circulated, and half the firm keeps a private copy that disagrees with it.

What changes

It becomes one live calendar the office reads and updates, on the same link every time.

06

The IT vendor bills for every email

So you think twice before asking, and small things stay broken for months.

What changes

We take those conversations. Your IT company, your software vendors, your accountant. You stay copied, so you always know where things stand.

These are some of the things we have run into. Every firm has something we have not seen before.

Why it never gets fixed

There is nobody whose job this is

Every hour in a firm is billable or it is overhead. Fixing how the work runs is neither.

So it lands on whoever is least able to say no, on top of everything they already do. It gets started in a quiet week. It does not get finished.

Most firms we meet did not plan the growth. Other firms stepped back, the work came to them, and the way the firm runs never caught up. Fifty people, still running like the twelve-person firm it used to be. One process laid on top of the last one, every one of them sensible when it was made.

You may have an administrator holding it together. Almost nobody has an operations person, with an operations background, whose whole job is how the work runs.

So you do the one thing that is easy to approve. You hire.

An administrator told us they had hired and it still was not enough. The volume kept climbing faster than the headcount. You cannot hire fast enough to catch that. At some point the only thing left to change is how the work runs.

By then people are burning out, and it is rarely the cases doing it. It is everything stacked around them.

Firms now name technology decisions as their biggest operational problem, ahead of the volume of work. The software is rarely the hard part. Choosing it, moving onto it and getting people to use it is a job of its own.

What changed is the other side of it. The tools that were built for firms of five hundred are now within reach of a firm of fifty, and they no longer cost a fortune. Most firms are already paying for more than they use.

You are not going to hire a project manager for this, and you should not have to. What you want is somebody who has done it before, for as long as it takes and no longer.

How we work

We listen first, then we build with your people

We start by sitting with the people who do the work. Half an hour each. We ask what took the most time this week, and they tell us things nobody has asked them before.

All of this runs remotely. Screen shares while people work, short calls, and one shared place for whatever we build. It means we watch the actual screen where the work happens, which is the thing you cannot see from a conference room. No travel on your invoice, and no day lost to hosting us. We come in person when there is a reason to.

We work alongside your administrator, or whoever is holding it together, and we build with the paralegals and assistants who will use it. Their version usually exists already, somewhere on somebody's desktop. We make it the standard, with their name on it.

Where it goes from there depends on your firm. Sometimes it is a set of small fixes and the week gets shorter. Sometimes it is the system decision that has been waiting years. We tell you which one you have, and we say why.

Nobody gets forced. If a partner wants to keep writing time on paper, that stays. We start with the people who want it.

What it is like

The first week

Every firm is different. The first week usually goes something like this.

Day 1

We get on a call and ask one question

What took the most time this week. You and one or two people from your team. We listen and take notes, and we do not pitch anything.

Day 2

We come back with what we would fix first

A short list, with what each one is costing you now and what it would take to change. You tell us whether it makes sense before we build a thing.

Day 3

We build the first versions

Usually the documents that get rebuilt every case, the scheduling that runs on email, and whatever is eating billable time without showing up anywhere. You see all of it before your team does.

Day 4

We sit with your people and try it on live work

Here is what it does, try it now, on a live file. Anything that feels wrong gets fixed while we are sitting there.

Day 5

We check what saved time

What worked, what did not, what comes next. The tools stay either way.

We recommend ninety days. That is how long a change takes to hold.

What the numbers look like

The math a partner will check

Six minutes of unbilled time a day, per timekeeper, at a $175 rate, across twenty timekeepers and 250 working days, is $87,500 a year.

That is an illustration. Few firms have run their own version of it. It is what hides inside “we enter time on Friday.”

The other number is the case management system. For a mid-sized firm the software alone can run into six figures over a multi-year term. It is the largest technology decision a firm makes, and it is usually made once a decade, by people carrying full caseloads.

That decision is worth preparing properly.

The smaller numbers are the ones that surprise people. In one firm the forms folder held seven thousand files. About eight hundred were still in use. Thirty were versions of the letterhead, and one of them was named Bad.
