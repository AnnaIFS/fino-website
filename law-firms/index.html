What the week looks like

You already know where the hours go

None of this is on anyone's list of problems. All of it costs the firm a day a week.

01

A paralegal emails to ask where a records request is

Then emails again on Thursday. One person becomes the status database for the whole firm, and answering counts as their job.

02

The trial calendar is a Word document

Someone maintains it by hand, it is out of date the day it is circulated, and half the firm keeps a private copy that disagrees with it.

03

Time gets entered on Friday for work done on Monday

Six minutes a day, per timekeeper, is a real number at the end of a year. Everyone knows it and nobody has time to fix it.

04

Every new case, someone rebuilds the same documents

From memory, or from whichever version was on somebody's desktop.

05

The IT vendor bills for every email

So you think twice before asking, and small things stay broken for months.

06

Your attorneys are already using AI

Nobody wrote the rules, and nobody wants to be the one who asks what has been pasted into a chat window.

Why it never gets fixed

There is nobody whose job this is

Every hour in a firm is billable or it is overhead. Fixing how the work runs is neither.

So it lands on whoever is least able to say no, on top of everything they already do. It gets started in a quiet week. It does not get finished.

Most firms we meet grew faster than the way they run. Fifty people, still operating like the twelve person firm it used to be, because nobody ever had a spare quarter to change it. So you do the one thing that is easy to approve. You hire.

An administrator told us they had hired and it still was not enough, because the volume kept climbing faster than the headcount ever could. That is the part that makes it unsustainable. You cannot hire your way level with something that is still rising, and at some point the only thing left to change is how the work runs.

By then people are burning out, and it is rarely the cases doing it. It is everything stacked around them.

Which is why firms now name technology decisions as their biggest operational problem, ahead of the volume of work itself. Not because the software is bad. Because choosing it, moving onto it and getting people to actually use it is a job, and in most firms it belongs to nobody.

You are not going to hire a project manager for this, and you should not have to. What you want is somebody who has done it before, for as long as it takes and no longer.

Right now that is a firm in the middle of exactly this. A shared scheduling system, a live trial calendar the whole office reads and updates, and the move onto a new case management system, which is the largest technology decision a firm makes and usually the one that has waited longest.

The arithmetic partners respond to

Six minutes of unbilled time a day, per timekeeper, at a $175 average rate, across twenty timekeepers, is roughly $100,000 a year.

That is an illustration rather than a promise, and almost nobody has calculated their own version of it. It is what hides inside “we enter time on Friday.”

The part nobody helps with

Getting it through the partners’ meeting

You can be right about the decision and still lose it in the room.

Partners want to see where their files will live, what a billing entry looks like on screen, and what happens to the calendar on their phone. Getting twenty minutes on their calendar takes two weeks, so the twenty minutes has to work.

We build the numbers, we get the vendor to demo their own product because they are better at it than we are, and we name the gaps out loud before anyone in the room finds them. A recommendation that hides a weakness does not survive the first partner who spots it.
